# Share Garden: the idea, and what it teaches

August 25, 2026, revised the same evening. The concept summary and the
learning outcomes mapping for the restarted Share Garden, settled with
Marlon across three question rounds after Ada's playtest
(docs/testing-feedback.md). Where this conflicts with docs/garden-prd.md,
this document wins and the PRD gets revised after approval.

The first draft's seed-everything model was corrected on Marlon's three
objections: stocks do not start from zero when you buy them, stocks are
not all the same size, and selling does not kill the company. The
corrections became the model.

## The idea

Your land sits beside a standing forest, the market: trees of every
species and size, alive before you arrived. On your own ground, three
kinds of thing to own, all native to land, none wearing a finance word:

- **Annual crops.** Seeded in spring, weathered all summer, harvested
  and sold in fall at that day's market price, and then the cycle ends.
  This is real crop farming, a real market in real life: gluts, weather
  risk, planning, quick money that finishes. Seeds are honest here
  because a wheat bed never pretended to be a company.
- **Bearing trees.** Bought standing, at whatever size they are now,
  from the forest beyond the fence. Every fall they fruit, and the
  harvest sells while the tree stays yours; the tree itself also has a
  standing price if you ever sell it whole. Income while you hold, and
  an asset that rises and falls on its own. Each fall the fruit offers
  the real choice: sell it at the stall, or plant its seeds and grow a
  new sapling of the same species. A stressed tree fruits thin before
  it fails. And the endgame milestone is the garden that feeds itself:
  the year fall's fruit covers the spring purse, the garden no longer
  needs your income to grow.
- **Great trees.** No fruit. Bought standing, at size. The tree IS the
  value: its size and vigor are its price, one ruler, readable at a
  glance.

**Growth is never a script.** Trees flourish and wither at any age with
the seasons and their species' fortunes. A giant can decline for years;
a sapling can shoot up or die. Nothing is guaranteed by youth and
nothing is protected by size.

**Buying and selling.** Buying moves a tree inside your fence at its
current size. Selling carts it back to the market, alive, and you keep
seeing it over the fence; if it keeps growing after you sold it, you
watch. Harvests sell at the stall, which never closes and always pays
the posted price, storm days included. The player never tends anything:
every choice is an ownership choice.

**The economy is its own.** A seeded agricultural simulation: weather
drives yields, supply and demand set prices, species differ in
hardiness, speed, and yield. Booms, gluts, blights, and recoveries
emerge from the sim, and the weather is indifferent to the player.

**The year turn.** Fall closes with the harvest and the season summary.
Winter is a held breath: nothing plants, the garden stands under snow,
and the snow picture is the year's shareable image. Spring opens with
the spring purse, a modest fixed sum arriving every year, good years
and bad; the market reprices (last year's boom species cost the most);
and over the years new species appear in the shop and the forest, which
is how the game widens choice without a tutorial.

**Sessions are compressed, the spine is long play.** A sitting plays a
few seasons with pauses at the decision moments. The garden persists
between sittings, because the product, in Ada's words, is something
casual she could feel proud of over time.

**Social is a picture.** Seeing how friends are doing means seeing
their gardens.

**Parked for a late chapter:** planting a tree from seed, cheap,
mostly fatal, occasionally a giant, which is venture investing and
deserves its own act, not the first verb.

## The learning outcomes mapping

Each row: the mechanism, what the player feels in garden terms, and the
principle it transfers to. The third column never appears in the game.

1. **Blight and the monoculture.** Blight strikes one species at a
   time; an all-one-crop garden dies whole and restarts, a mixed one
   loses a bed and stands. Ada's own words: she would learn to
   diversify because one blight would force her to restart. Transfers
   to: diversification.
2. **Three kinds on one land.** Quick cycles, income producers, and
   growers, balanced however the player likes, legible by silhouette.
   Transfers to: asset allocation.
3. **Keep the tree, sell the fruit.** Income arrives every fall while
   the asset stays yours and keeps its own price. Transfers to: yield,
   income while invested, total return.
4. **Sell the fruit or plant its seeds.** Every basket is the choice
   between coins now and a new sapling of the same species. Transfers
   to: consuming returns against reinvesting them; compounding.
5. **Uprooting a bearing tree.** One payment against years of fruit,
   both numbers in the open, and someone else picks it over the fence
   afterward. Transfers to: principal against income; the golden goose.
6. **Thin fruit on a stressed tree.** The payout falls before the tree
   does. Transfers to: a shrinking payout is a warning worth reading.
7. **The garden that feeds itself.** The year the fruit covers the
   spring purse. Transfers to: living off returns.
8. **The tree over the fence.** A sold tree lives on in the market
   forest, visibly still growing if it grows. Transfers to: selling a
   winner ends your share of its compounding.
9. **Saplings and giants.** Cheap, quick, fragile against dear, slow,
   steady, priced by size in one glance. Transfers to: risk and
   reward, small and large, by silhouette.
10. **Giants wither too.** Old great trees can decline for seasons on
   end while a young tree overtakes them. Transfers to: size and past
   growth guarantee nothing.
11. **Storm prices.** The stall still buys mid-storm, at the worst
   prices of the year; the summary shows what held beds recovered to.
   Transfers to: panic selling.
12. **Prices chase last year.** The boom species costs the most to buy
   this spring and gluts by fall. Transfers to: chasing performance.
13. **The spring purse.** The same sum arrives every year, and planting
   it after a brutal year buys more than planting it after a boom.
   Transfers to: investing from income, dollar cost averaging.
14. **A species can die out.** Gone for good, the stall stops buying.
    Transfers to: things go to zero.
15. **Goals and ruin.** Season goals are garden things, never coin
    targets, and losing a garden you were proud of plants the next one
    differently. Transfers to: wealth as ownership, and loss felt once,
    remembered.

## The mapping, in one breath

Teaching investing through gardening.

- plants -> stocks
- many plants, so one blight cannot take everything -> diversification
- bearing trees, steady fruit every fall -> bonds and their coupons
- great trees, held for years -> long-term growth
- the farmers market, always open -> the stock market
- price variability per crop -> price variability per asset

How explicit are we that this is a stock: the tagline says it (investing
taught through gardening, on the box from day one), the gameplay never
says it, and the end of year one says it once, this list on one card,
then never again. Outcomes are still measured from the play log, not
quizzed: mix widening after a first blight, holding through storms, the
purse planted in down years.

## Open to settle before the PRD revision

- The economy sim's rules: yield curves, supply and demand at the
  stall, blight odds per species, and the two markets per bearing
  species (fruit price and tree price) staying coherent.
- Blight tuning: weather, not punishment.
- What harvest pauses offer beyond sell or hold (a granary for storing
  a harvest is commodity inventory, maybe a later idea).
- Whether bearing and great trees are separate species or life stages
  of the same ones.
- From the simulated teen playtest (Aug 25, kept out of the real tester
  log): great trees need a genuine edge over fruit trees or nobody buys
  one; the reveal card must use experience-first words (trees that pay
  you to keep them, before any word like bond) and should only name
  what this player actually did, built from their own log; and the
  middle of a season needs real decision density, since a game about
  not touching anything is hard to want to open.
