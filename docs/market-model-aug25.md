# The market model, August 25 2026

Supersedes docs/tycoon-ideas-aug25.md. Written after Marlon rejected the
register split (owning a share does not pay you a cut of what the company
makes) and named the two unsolved problems: who sets the price, and why
holding beats flipping. Dividends and index funds are out of scope
entirely. Pizza and tycoon are candidate skins only, not the concept.

Carried forward from the superseded doc: the unit law (ownership units are
fixed and countable, businesses grow by visible accumulation, cash is
stacks of fixed bills) and every settled filter (five word pitch, visible
cause, entirely new concepts, compressed long play or hypercasual,
progressive disclosure, never overly educational).

## The core model: the market is a crowd you can see

One idea answers both open problems at once. Under each asset stands a
small crowd of buyers, each holding an offer. The price IS the best offer
in the crowd. Nobody computes it. It is what someone standing there will
pay, which is the true answer to who sets a price.

The crowd watches the same thing the player watches. When the underlying
thing does well, the crowd thickens and offers climb. When it does badly,
offers sag. When it collapses, the crowd walks away, and that image is
what a crash actually is: not a number going down, buyers leaving.

Jared's three questions under this model:

1. A portfolio is your countable units of different things, each with its
   own crowd underneath it.
2. Change over time is the underlying thing visibly performing, and the
   crowd responding to what it just saw.
3. Buying is paying what a seller in the crowd asks. Selling is taking
   what a buyer in the crowd offers. Both are always available, at the
   crowd's number, never yours.

## Why holding beats flipping, three visible teeth, no dividends needed

1. **The gap.** Buyers offer a little less than sellers ask. Every flip
   crosses that gap twice, and the game shows it plainly: you bought at
   $12, the best offer the moment after is $11. Churning bleeds by the
   gap, which is the true reason churning bleeds. Level one keeps the gap
   flat at $1 so the lesson is arithmetic a kid can do.
2. **The drift.** Underlying performance compounds across a run. Minutes
   are noise, seasons are growth. Flippers trade the noise and pay the gap
   for it; holders ride the drift for free.
3. **Panic pricing.** Dips are exactly when the crowd is thin. Selling
   into one means accepting the worst offer of the week from the only
   buyer left. Waiting visibly refills the crowd. The player learns why
   panic selling loses by watching who they would be selling to.

What is deliberately deferred, not denied: WHY buyers care about
performance (the claim on future profits, where dividends live) and
expectations running ahead of reality (bubbles, the headline layer).
Both are progressive disclosure, not core.

## Skins, scored

The concept needs an underlying thing whose performance a kid reads at a
glance, and a crowd whose interest in it feels natural. Candidates:

**The League. "Own pieces of sports teams."**
Teams play a visible season; form, streaks, and the table are the
fundamentals, and no audience on earth reads standings faster than kids.
The crowd of buyers is fans in the stands. Slumps teach holding, spreads
teach flipping's cost, relegation or scandal is the crash, diversification
is owning across clubs because any club can slump. Season structure IS the
loop: goals, harder divisions, carry over.
Honest flaw: wins are zero sum across the league, and the real market is
not. Mitigable (the league's whole audience can grow), but it is the
concept's one lie under pressure.

**The Card Table. "Trade cards other kids want."**
Trading cards are the one childhood experience where everyone already
knows the price is set by what other kids will pay. Binder = portfolio,
instantly. The card's athlete performs in the background as the
fundamental. Honest flaw: cards produce nothing, so the frame flirts with
greater fool collecting; the fundamentals have to carry it or it teaches
hype.

**The Scene. "Back bands as they grow."**
Bands practice, play bigger rooms, grow a following; followings are not
zero sum, so the whole scene can rise, which matches the real market
better than sports. The crowd of buyers is scene people. Honest flaw:
audience growth as a fundamental is closer to attention than to output,
and attention is the speculation trap wearing a leather jacket.

**The Street, light. "Own slices of pizza shops."**
The prior skin, minus tycoon economics: shops just visibly do business,
the investor crowd watches and offers. Customer counts are not zero sum
and are output, not attention. Honest flaw: after the register version,
the shop frame carries baggage, and it is the least novel of the four.

## Where this leaves it

The model is settled enough to test; the skin is not. The League and the
Card Table are the strongest and their flaws are opposite: the League has
the best fundamentals and loop but a zero sum world; the Card Table has
the truest crowd intuition but the weakest fundamentals. The Scene and
the Street are alive as fallbacks. Sketches wait until Marlon picks.

## Addendum, same day: the boid market (Marlon's riff)

Marlon proposed, mid-round: a top-down black and white map in the Crowd
City vein, plots are companies, boid crowds gather where the hype is and
the crowd density is the price; when something happens people drain away
and what you can get falls with them. Second image: companies as stacks
of bricks, a brick is a share, everyone carries a cash stack, you take
bricks and build your own isometric stack.

Synthesis: these are one game. Bricks answer the portfolio and unit
questions, boids answer the price question, and two holding teeth turn
physical: selling requires a person to hand the brick to (a deserted plot
is illiquidity you can see), and walking time is a transaction cost that
makes churning slow by construction.

The danger to design against: crowd-as-price alone teaches front-running
the herd. The anchor: every company shows its own real activity, the
crowd is drawn by it but overshoots both ways, and the skill is reading
the building through the mob. Lesson in five words: watch the shop, not
the crowd.

Settled in the follow-up round: one game (bricks are the shares, boids
are the price), the player is a figure in the crowd (walking time is the
cost of churning), and activity anchors the crowd.

Brick value representation (Marlon's question, answered by the unit law):
a brick never changes size. Its value is the going rate at its shop,
shown as a physical pile of bills on a stand at each plot, one brick buys
this pile; the pile rises as the crowd thickens, sinks as it thins, and a
crash is a bare stand with nobody around it. The player's own brick stack
carries a ghost bill pile showing what the whole stack fetches right now.
Height is dollars only ever in cash. Cost basis tabs on bricks are a
later disclosure layer.
