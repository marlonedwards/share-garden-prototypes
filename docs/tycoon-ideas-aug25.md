## The unit law

Marlon's pizza objection ("slices should not grow") generalizes, and it
explains a past failure. Real world units are fixed. A pizza slice, a coin,
a brick: when one of those inflates on screen the metaphor lies, because
nothing in the player's physical experience inflates. The coins direction
died here: Jared's rule 2 had coin thickness growing, and that is the part
that never sat right.

Real businesses do not grow by inflating. They grow by ADDING things: more
ovens, more customers, more trucks, more coins in the register. So the
honest tycoon representation is:

- Your unit of ownership is fixed and countable (a slice, a share flag).
- The business grows by visible accumulation, which is also the financially
  true picture (earn, reinvest, expand).
- Your holding's value is read from the size of the business times your
  fraction, and from the posted price tag, never from your unit swelling.
- Height is dollars survives where it belongs: cash is always a stack of
  fixed bills, and selling visibly converts your unit into that stack.

## The scoreboard

Jared's three core model questions:

1. Represent a bunch of stocks in a portfolio.
2. Show stocks changing over time.
3. Buy and sell.

Jared's foolproof test: one stock, many stocks, growth, decline,
diversification, crash, all legible at a glance. Jared's loop test: goal,
difficulty curve, progression.

Marlon's filters, current: five word pitch, visible cause for every price
move, the score rewards owning rather than trading (no timing bounties),
diversity and growth representable together, progressive disclosure room
(dividends, index funds, fees later), compressed long play or hypercasual.

## Baseline, why the shipped builds miss

- Orb: diversification legible, growth and decline not. Fails foolproof.
- Stack cylinder: growth legible, price moves causeless. Invites timing.
- Coins: units inflate. Fails the unit law. Marlon never liked it.
- Trigger and Floor: the verb is timing the tape. The score literally
  rewards speculation, the thing we are trying not to teach.
- Monkey: comparison frame, no ownership fantasy, scrapped.

Nothing shipped so far has ever put the CAUSE of a price move on screen.
That is the one genuinely new thing the tycoon frame buys us.

## Three core models in the tycoon space

### Model A: fraction of a visible business

Your unit is a fraction (a slice). The shop is on screen doing business.
Value is the shop's visible size times your fraction; a price tag keeps it
honest. Return comes mostly from the shop growing.

**Pizza Street. "Own slices of pizza shops."**
A street of shops, each pizza cut into eight fixed slices. Busy shop: line
out the door, ovens added, the counter price of a slice climbs. Empty shop:
price falls. A boarded up shop is a slice tagged zero, the slice itself
never changes size. The counter person always trades at the posted price.
Portfolio is your pizza box: slices stamped with shop logos, tags summing
at the box lid. Loop: each level is a season with a net worth goal, later
streets add recessions and fads.
Weak spots: value reads in two places (shop plus tag), and pure price
appreciation still smells faintly of buy low sell high. Fixable by giving
shops a visible register cut paid to slice holders, which bridges to B.

### Model B: claim on a visible cash flow

Your unit is a share of a machine or vehicle that visibly earns. Dividends
are the core, not the garnish. You buy for the catch, not the flip, so
timing is structurally beside the point. This is also the actual Roblox
tycoon loop (earn, collect, reinvest, expand) with ownership fractions
layered in.

**The Fleet. "Buy shares of fishing boats."**
A harbor. Each boat sails out and returns with a visible catch, the catch
sells, and the money splits across that boat's share flags, yours drop into
your pile as fixed bills. Boat price at the harbor office follows its catch
record, cause on screen. A good boat buys a bigger net (growth by
accumulation). Storms are forecast imperfectly; a boat can sink, the
sharpest crash image we have ever had, and the players who spread their
flags across boats sail on. Loop: seasons with an income goal, rougher
seas per level. Disclosure: harbor fund (a little of every boat) as the
index, insurance as bonds, sinking as bankruptcy.
Weak spots: sinking must be rare and partly forecast or it reads as slot
machine punishment. Nautical theming is less everyday than food.

**Arcade. "Own shares of arcade machines."**
One room, machines earning coin by coin as kids play. Fads move the crowd
between machines, cause on screen, no storms needed. A machine goes Out of
Order (crash). Same income split core as the fleet in a friendlier room.
Weak spot: machine popularity can look like fashion guessing, which drifts
back toward speculation unless upgrades drive the fads.

**Food Truck Row.** Same core, trucks and lunch lines. A skin, not a
separate model. Keep in the pocket.

### Model C: build a structure yourself

Tower stacking, sandcastle materials. Scored honestly: the structure is
YOURS, so nothing on screen is a company someone else runs. Fails Jared's
question 1 outright, and crash by toppling punishes dexterity, not
concentration. Sandcastle's cheap sand versus stone is a genuinely good
risk image, worth stealing as a material choice INSIDE a model A or B game,
not as its own game.

## Recommendation

Model C is out as a standalone. Mock the two strongest, one from each
surviving model, ultra simple, lab style like the August 7 cylinder round:

1. **The Fleet**, the cleanest answer to every tenet at once, and the only
   concept where speculation is structurally pointless rather than merely
   discouraged.
2. **Pizza Street**, the most familiar image (Jared himself said pizza as
   pie chart), with a register cut added so holders earn while holding.

Each mock is one screen, no engine: three businesses, fake ticking
fundamentals, buy, watch, sell, cash as fixed bill stacks. Judge them on
whether a cold viewer can answer Jared's three questions unprompted.
