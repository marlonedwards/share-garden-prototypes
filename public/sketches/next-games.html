Concept round, August 13

The next games: real money, proven forms

Four candidates. Every one is literally about investing: real stocks, real dollars, real dividends. No metaphors, no translation layer, no tutorial. Each one borrows a game form players already know, so it reads in one look. The market data underneath is our real era engine in all four.

The filter every candidate had to pass

  • 1Fun as a game for someone with zero interest in finance. The lesson is a side effect, never the point.
  • 2No metaphors. The things on screen are stocks, dollars, and dividends, called what they are.
  • 3Understandable from one look, because the form is already in your head.
  • 4Free to play badly. The game never blocks a mistake; it just lets the consequence arrive.
  • 5Real time. The world ticks whether you act or not.

1. The Big Board

Your idea, drawn twice. A Monopoly-shaped ring of stocks. Tokens stroll the board continuously, no dice and no turns; you make a decision when your token arrives somewhere. Three plain CPU tokens walk the same ring, buying by simple habits you can watch. Payday corner pays salary and dividends. A run is ten laps, one year per lap. At the final bell the highest net worth wins. The two mocks differ in one thing only: what the economy underneath is.

Variant A. Spaces are companies at real era prices that move every lap; the board itself is the market. You stop on a space, you may buy shares at today's price, and that is the whole interaction. Payday pays salary plus real dividends from everything you hold. No rent: your money grows because the companies do, and the ladder in the middle is the only scoreboard. In 2008 the banks row goes red and the board visibly gets cheaper; whether that scares you or excites you is the lesson arriving.

Variant B. Pure Monopoly economics wearing stock names: fixed prices, and when a rival token lands on a stock you own, they buy in and you collect rent for having been there first. Maximum familiarity and the rent moment feels great, but the teaching is shallower (own assets, collect income, manage cash) and the numbers are invented, which walks away from our every-price-is-real spine. The strongest possible body, the weakest possible truth.

A third path exists: variant A's real economy with variant B's rent moment reframed honestly (a rival landing on your stock buys shares and their broker fee routes to you as the market maker). Flagging it, not endorsing it; it needs a fun test, not a debate.

2. Hold

The one-button game. You start every round already invested: $10,000 in one real stock or the index, in a real dramatic year. The tape plays. The only button is SELL. That is the entire game: a staring contest with a real market, and the button is right there, big and red, daring you.

This was March 2009. Most players sold on day 43.
Tomorrow's round: a new real year, revealed only at the end. Some years, selling early was the survivable move; the game never says which kind you are in.
Why this works

The form: the do-not-press-the-button game, plus the daily-puzzle rhythm of Wordle. Everyone plays the same hidden year each day; compare with friends after.

Playing badly is playing: selling on the scary headline is always allowed, instantly executed, and frozen at that price for the reveal. Nobody scolds you. The chart just keeps going without you.

Honest: real days, real headlines that always arrive after the move they explain, no prediction rewarded. Some daily years reward the seller; history decides, not the designer.

Scope: the smallest build on this page. One tape, one button, one reveal screen.

3. Every Payday

The tap game. Money arrives on a rhythm: $100 lands in your cash tray every few seconds, a payday every game-month. One BUY button puts the tray into the index at today's real price. You can slam it every payday, or hoard cash waiting for a dip that may never come. The market ticks either way.

Your waiting won $3 a share this decade. Play 1974 or 2008 tomorrow and the same patience loses. The game keeps your lifetime record on both sides of the line.
Why this works

The form: a rhythm tapper. The satisfaction of the buy press (tray empties, share counter clicks up, dot lands on the chart) is the whole dopamine loop.

Playing badly is playing: hoard forever, market-time everything. The end screen never argues; it just prints your average cost next to the steady payday buyer's, and keeps score across your lifetime of runs.

Honest: real decades, and whether timing beat rhythm depends entirely on which real decade you drew. Over many runs the record tells its own story.

Risk: close to Hold in spirit; the two might be one game with two modes.

4. Payout

The idle game, played straight. Four real places money can sit: savings, bonds, the index fund, single stocks. Each one visibly drips what it actually pays. Cash lands in the tray; you route it. That is all, and it is the exact loop that made AdVenture Capitalist compulsive, except every number is a real yield doing what it really does.

Why this works

The form: idle numbers-go-up, the most proven hypercasual loop there is. The reinvest gesture (drag tray onto a row) is the only verb.

Playing badly is playing: shove everything into Rocket Motors because its good days are thrilling. Some runs that pays off. Then a real 2008 arrives on the tape and the row craters while savings drips on, one cent at a time.

Honest: yields and market rows run on the real era engine; the single stock is a real company's real path. No invented jackpots.

What leaks through: compounding (the per-second number grows because you fed it), and the risk ladder, lived rather than named.

Risk: idle games reward leaving, which fights the 5 to 15 minute session shape. Needs the speed dial tuned in hand.
