The game is right. The screen has no character yet.

The build plays correctly and looks like nothing: too much air, columns like toothpicks, every object floating on its own. Before anything is rebuilt, here is one second of the game drawn three ways. Same chapter, same turn, same seven cards, same real prices. What changes is the character, and the character is the thing to pick.

The moment, held still

September 2008 has just resolved. The wall stood at twenty four blocks on the October 2007 record and it stands at seventeen now, and not one card left the table. That is the whole metaphor in a single frame, which is why all three directions draw exactly this moment and nothing else.

The composition, cut down

Every price here is the real monthly close from src/data/eraGfc.json, and every block count is that month's worth divided by $80 and rounded down. The seven cards were bought in January 2007, March 2007 and October 2007, so the wall is what those purchases were actually worth, month by month.

Direction A. The sketch, kept

This is the sketch's own language with nothing loosened: warm off-white, a lit stage for the wall, flat blocks with the one pixel highlight, white cards with suit rings, one gold line for the target. What changed is the shape of the board. A wall that has to hold forty blocks and only twenty one months is a portrait object, so the board turned portrait with it, and the sprawl had nowhere left to live.

Press End turn to resolve turn six into March 2009: six months land one column at a time, the count steps down a digit at a time, and the board returns to where it was after a beat. Press Market to bring the eight cards up over the wall, and hover the rainbow stack. The pale rules across the stage are the wall's counting grid, one line every five blocks, so a glance gives you a number without counting to it.

Direction B. The counting house

Here the front page stops being one screen and becomes the whole game. Cream paper, a serif for every name and every sentence, hairline rules, and a wall that lies down as a ledger: one dated entry per resolved turn, blocks run out to the right, total in the margin. The target is the first entry on the page, ruled in gold, and the four turns you have not played are already ruled and waiting for their numbers.

A row is easier to compare than a column, because the eye reads lengths against a shared left edge, and a target sitting at the top as an outlined row turns the gap into arithmetic. The cost is real and it is stated below: blocks stop stacking up, so growth reads as reaching rather than rising. Press End turn to write the March 2009 entry as it lands, and Market to see the market as a classified column.

Direction C. The instrument

Character from density instead of decoration. Ink on off-white, one strict grid, no rounded corners, and no colour anywhere except the blocks and the suit rings. A turn is laid three blocks wide so the blocks can be as large as they are, the count is set in numerals you could read across a room, and the cards fall back to slim chips carrying name, ring, worth and price.

One column per turn is what the spec already says the wall does, and at this size a turn is a brick you could count from the sofa. The scale down the left counts in fives, the heavy rule is the target at thirty seven, and the four channels on the right are the turns still to play. Press End turn, and Market for the eight cards as a two column list.

What each one costs

what it argueswhat it buyswhat it costs
A. The sketch The drawing was never the problem. The layout was. Nothing to relearn, the finest grain of history on screen, and a crash you can see as a shape in one look. It is the safest of the three and the least memorable, and at eight pixels a block the wall is a texture before it is a count. A portrait board wastes a wide display.
B. The counting house Money has a print tradition, and this game is a record of what happened. The most character by a distance, blocks big enough to count, and a target you compare by length instead of by height. Growth stops rising, which weakens the one thing a wall does better than a chart. Serif at small sizes needs care on low resolution screens.
C. The instrument Confidence is the character. Take everything else away. The biggest blocks, the loudest number, the calmest screen, and colour that only ever means something. It reads as a tool before it reads as a toy, and a nine year old may find it stern. A turn laid three wide breaks counting in fives, so the printed number has to carry the precision.

What this breaks in the spec, said plainly

Section 6.1 fixes four regions forever, and the third of them is a market strip that is always on screen. All three directions delete that region and summon the market instead, because a strip of eight cards you are not buying this turn was the largest single source of the sprawl.

The honest cost is that a market you cannot see is a market that could lie about what is available, and section 3.3 rests on never lying. Two things hold the line. The affordance always carries the count, so the number of buyable cards is on screen at all times, and the drawer opens over the wall inside region B, where every other overlay already lives. The four region law becomes a three region law, and 6.1 gets rewritten rather than quietly ignored.

Two honest notes about the numbers

The read line says seven blocks, not fourteen. At $80 a block, seven cards cost fourteen blocks in total, so fourteen blocks could only leave a wall that had roughly tripled first. Seven is what these real prices actually took: October 2007 to September 2008 turned $1,958.94 into $1,403.40.

Chapter six needs turns of different lengths. Turns one to four run two or three months each through 2007 so the record high gets its own beat, turn five carries the whole fall from October 2007 to September 2008, and the last four turns cover the recovery in years. Direction A keeps drawing one column per month underneath, which is why turn five landed eleven columns at once.

The question for you

Pick the character first and the rebuild follows it. If B wins, the ledger becomes the wall and the front page stops being a special screen. If C wins, the colour budget shrinks to blocks and rings and the whole app gets quieter. If A wins, the answer was composition all along, and the work is a week of tightening rather than a new visual system.
