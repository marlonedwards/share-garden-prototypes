Share Garden · The Tally · design sketch · 10 August 2026

Cards are what you own. Blocks are what it is worth.

The tile chart fixed the picture and left the game behind it unfinished. This is the game: a solitaire built out of real market history, where every asset is a card you can stack and every dollar is a block on the wall behind the table. Splitting those two units is the whole idea, and it settles the one metaphor test the orb and the cylinder both failed.

The exhibit: a crash takes blocks and leaves every card

Jared's sixth test was crash and recovery: you still own the same things, they are worth less. A single shape can never show that, because the shape is the only object on screen. Two units can. Move the year.

The card

The ring is what the money does. The pip is what the company sells. Both are true groupings, and together they mean nothing on screen ever has to say the word diversify.

Rings: the asset class

Savings. Grows slowly, never falls.
Lending. You lent it, they pay you back.
Ownership. A slice of one company.
Baskets. One card that holds many.
Speculation. A price with nothing behind it yet.

The basket ring is the only radial gradient in the game and the only card that shines on hover. It is not mechanically wild. It is just the one card whose ring is every colour at once, because it is the one card that is every colour at once. Hover it above.

Pips: the sector

The pip earns its place on exactly one day. In September 2008 every card carrying a bank pip loses most of its worth in the same resolve. A player who spread across five ownership cards that all happen to be banks watches all five fall together, and nothing has to say a word.

Sector correlation is real in the data we already ship. We are not inventing it. We are making it visible.

The board never moves

The current build is three cards that swap out on every beat, so nothing feels like one place. The fix is structural: four regions, fixed heights, forever. Every overlay renders inside the wall panel, over a scrim, with the market and the table still visible and still correct underneath. Press the buttons and watch nothing below region B move by a pixel.

Rules that hold at every size

  • 1The four regions never reorder, never collapse, and never change height at any phase of any turn.
  • 2Every overlay lives inside region B. The run over report is the only one allowed to take the viewport, and only after a beat.
  • 3The market and the table scroll sideways. Nothing wraps to a second line.
  • 4A block is always square. The wall decides how many turns one drawn column covers so the blocks stay square, which is the logic already built and working.
  • 5Everything is vector. No raster background art, nothing that softens on a large display.
  • 6The whole game fits one viewport at 1280 by 800 with no vertical scroll, and no horizontal scroll from 360 pixels up.

Page is warm off-white. The wall is a single panel a few shades deeper, so the tiles read as objects in a lit case. Not a dark app. The pixel language lives in the tiles only; no pixel typeface anywhere.

A turn, and the resolve that pays for it

Fully turn-based. There is no play button, no speed control, and nothing that can be waited out. Nothing moves until you commit, and then the resolve is the payoff: blocks land one at a time with weight, and the count steps a digit at a time. No number ever tweens. Counting is the pleasure.

Income arrives every turn, in every chapter, so no turn is a skip and dollar cost averaging is the default rather than a strategy. Four beats, always the same order. Plan, and nothing is committed. Commit, one button. Resolve, about 1.2 seconds, skippable by tapping. Read, one line in the header rail. A player should be able to clear a chapter without reading a single sentence and still have learned something by the end of it.

The unit is earned, never automatic

The wall never goes over forty blocks, so it stays countable, which means the unit has to grow. It grows exactly four times, on its own screen, between chapters, never inside one. Four blocks fuse into one, and four cards of the same name fuse into one card, so a card is always two blocks and the economy never shifts under you.

The ruler changing is not a rendering problem to hide. It is the moment a kid feels how much bigger the number got, and it is free curriculum. It is also the only time the game ever changes the meaning of anything, which is why it gets a whole screen and a button press.

History that nobody has to read

The teaching layer stays. It stops being a wall of text. A dated moment resolves as a front page inside the wall panel: three headlines, each one line on its face, each opening to at most two sentences on tap. Nobody is ever made to read more than one line to continue.

Headlines stay verbatim from named publications on named dates, per the honesty rule already in the course style contract. The definition sentences that currently sit in the gate cards move to the field guide, which unlocks the moment a concept first appears in play. A player who wants the textbook sentence gets it in two taps. A player who does not, never sees it. The old gate buttons are gone, because the decision now lives in the turn, where the money is.

The ladder, the target, and the run

A chapter is a level with a block target on the wall from turn one. Clear it or the run ends. The first run is a whole life in order, and clearing a chapter unlocks it permanently as a start point, so later runs begin deeper.

Eight chapters

1The piggy bank. A pile that does nothing stays the same size. Ninety seconds, unfailable, no decision in it.$5
2Savings. One card that grows on its own, slowly, and never falls.$5
3Prices. A price moves. Your card count does not move with it.$20
4Two names. The first real choice, and one of the two falls hard.$20
5The fund. One card that holds all of them, and it is not the fastest one.$80
6The panic. 2008 on real prices. A whole sector falls together.$80
7The mania. What a price with nothing behind it eventually does.$320
8The long run. Forty years, very few moves, and the unit upgrades twice.$320

Chapters one to five use illustrative numbers, the way the existing intro lessons use Maya's lemonade stand, and say so on their chapter card. Six to eight use the real datasets already shipped.

The target is a floor, not a corridor

The old corridor had a ceiling, and a ceiling reads as "investing well is wrong", which is not a thing we believe. The target is a number of blocks to reach, and nothing ever penalises finishing rich.

A chapter's target is about 95% of what buying the basket on turn one and never touching it would have produced.

That one rule does the teaching by arithmetic. Cash never clears a chapter after the second. Tracking the market clears every chapter with a small margin. One concentrated name clears a chapter spectacularly sometimes and misses badly more often, because that is what the real prices do. Over eight chapters the odds compound.

Concentration wins rounds. Spreading wins runs.

Balatro's ante structure does the pedagogy for us, so no rule ever has to.

A forensic report, not a scold. It names what you did and what it cost, from your own trade log, in blocks. It shows the alternative you could see at the time and let go, once. It never says you should have, and it never scores you against it. One miss ends a run, and there are no retries with known prices, because replaying a known tape rewards memorising the future.

The collector's box

Modelled on the Orb's silhouetted achievement slots: you can see the shape of everything you have not earned, which is most of the pull. Badges are where spreading is rewarded, and they sit deliberately outside the scoring loop, so no turn is ever won by obeying a rule.

Three shelves: instruments, badges, eras. Nothing is ever announced mid-turn. Badges land on the chapter summary, one at a time, with a small flip. Compounding gets its reward here rather than on the card face, as the long hold badge: finish a run still holding a card you bought in the first chapter you played.

The reward function contract

Insider Trading, the Balatro-for-stocks game released this February, is a good game and a terrible teacher: the optimal play is to dump, crash the price, and buy the dip, and reviewers finish it saying they still do not understand the market. Theming a deckbuilder in stocks teaches nothing by itself. These are the rules a verifier checks hardest, and a change that breaks one is rejected however well it plays.

  • 1No move may move a price. The player is a price taker in every chapter.
  • 2No reward, badge, bonus or unlock may pay for trading volume, for timing, or for a correct prediction.
  • 3Nothing is scored against a counterfactual. The run over screen may show an option you passed on, once. It may never rank your choice against it.
  • 4No baseline bonus for holding several suits. Spreading is rewarded by the real prices, and outside the loop by a badge.
  • 5Every unlock is an asset or a habit. Never a discount, never an edge, never information nobody had at the time.
  • 6Every number a card shows existed on that date.
  • 7A chapter can never be cleared by an action that would be bad practice with real money. If playtesting finds one, the target gets retuned. The rule does not get relaxed.

What to build, in order

  • 1The card model: suits, pips, lots, stone cards. Then the eight chapters with their targets, stakes and units.
  • 2The four region shell, with the overlay layer inside region B. This is the thing that decides whether it feels like one place, so it comes before content.
  • 3The wall, refactored from the tile stage that already works, plus the card face with its rings and its one radial gradient.
  • 4The turn: drag to buy, drag to discard, commit, resolve, read. Build the resolve with the care a Balatro hand gets, because it is the whole payoff.
  • 5Chapters one to five, which are illustrative and cheap. Play them before touching a real era.
  • 6Chapters six to eight on the real datasets, then calibrate every target against the buy-the-basket rule.
  • 7The collector's box, the badges, and the front door card. The Orb stays as game two.

Open in build: whether chapter seven is dotcom or crypto, where forty years of data for chapter eight comes from, whether income arrives every turn or only at chapter start, and whether one miss ending a run is too harsh at chapter seven. All four are written down rather than quietly resolved.
