Change the stage, not the game

The orb and the cylinder both put value in volume, the channel people read worst, and showed one shape at a time, so there was nothing to compare against. This is the alternative: a portfolio drawn as countable blocks. The first sketch is not a new game, it is a new stage under the game that already exists at /stack. The other two are chapters that would sit around it.

The two tests, stated before the ideas

Jared gave two critiques on 6 August. Turning them into pass/fail lists first means nothing gets to be judged on how pretty it is.

Metaphor test

The picture has to be foolproof for all six of these, using a physical experience a twelve year old already has.

  1. 1Growth reads instantly, without a number
  2. 2Decline same, and it must sting
  3. 3Many stocks composition is visible at a glance
  4. 4One stock looks obviously fragile before anything bad happens
  5. 5Diversification visible as a change over time, not just right now
  6. 6Crash and recovery you still own the same things, they are worth less

Gamification test

The Candy Crush skeleton, plus one rule the reference games do not have.

  1. 1A clear objective per level
  2. 2Constrained moves scarcity is where decisions live
  3. 3Escalating difficulty
  4. 4Visible progression between sessions
  5. 5A fail state that teaches and doubles as the assessment
  6. 6A share artifact worth showing someone
  7. 7The reward function teaches the truth the one that Insider Trading fails
Insider Trading, the Balatro-for-stocks game released this February, is a good game and a terrible teacher: the optimal play is to dump everything, crash the price, and buy the dip. Reviewers finish it saying they still do not understand the market. Theming a deckbuilder in stocks teaches nothing by itself. The reward function is the curriculum.

The substrate

One block is a fixed amount of money. A column is one month. Colour is which company. Band order never changes, so blue is always on the floor and you read composition by position as well as by hue. Scrub it, switch the portfolio, and change what a block is worth.

What the block value control is really for

This is the one thing that can kill the whole idea, so it is the first control on the page. Blocks only teach if you can count them. Set a block to $10 and by year five the column is a hundred blocks tall and you are back to reading a shape, which is the orb's failure in a different costume. Set it to $100 and the early game moves in invisible steps.

The answer I would build: a block has a fixed value for a long stretch, and when the column gets too tall the game stops, says "from now on every block is $100," and visibly fuses four blocks into one. The ruler changing is not a rendering problem to hide. It is the moment a kid feels how much bigger the number got, and it is free curriculum.

One stage swap, and two chapters that could follow

These are not three prototypes to choose between. Presenting another choose-one-of-three is the pattern that has been getting rejected. The first is a component swap inside the game that already exists; the other two are optional chapters that would reuse it.

Sketch one · the drop-in
The Stack, re-staged

Not a new game. The loop at /stack already works: a goal corridor around the index, the three behaviour stars, scouting cards, real eras, bankruptcy seams. The only thing that fails Jared's first critique is the picture. So change the picture and keep everything else.

The same run, drawn in blocks instead of a cylinder. The goal corridor becomes a countable line, the history stays on screen instead of being thrown away every frame, and you can see your own composition drift toward one colour long before the crash arrives.
What actually changes in the codebase:
  1. StackStage.tsx gets a sibling, TileStage.tsx, taking the identical props: bands, cash, indexValue, goalLine, corridor, maxDollars.
  2. Height stops being a continuous cylinder and becomes a count of blocks, at a stated dollar value each.
  3. The stage keeps a column per step instead of redrawing one shape, so the run has a visible past.
  4. The monotonic ruler you already keep in the parent becomes the block denomination, which is the same problem you have already solved once.
  5. Everything above the stage, the scenario flow, the stars, the gates, the quiz, is untouched.
Metaphor test
  • yesGrowth is blocks appearing. Decline is blocks falling off. Both countable, which continuous height never was.
  • yesComposition is the colour of the current column, read bottom up.
  • yesOne stock is a solid column. It looks wrong next to everyone else's before anything happens.
  • yesDiversification drifts visibly across the whole chart, which is the thing the orb could never show.
  • yesCrash removes blocks and keeps every colour. You own the same things, worth less.
Gamification test
  • yesAlready built and already tested: finish inside the corridor around the index.
  • yesAlready built. Trade scarcity and the era gates are the engineered temptations.
  • yesLevels and eras exist. The corridor tightens as the eras get nastier.
  • yesThe three behaviour stars are the progression, and they reward habits rather than returns.
  • yesFalling out of the corridor shows your blocks beside the index. That screenshot is the assessment.
  • watchShare artifact improves a lot: a block chart screenshots far better than a cylinder, but it is still less of a trophy than a Balatro score.
Verdict. This is the whole proposal, and it is a day of work rather than a rebuild. Every gamification item is already shipped, so the change is aimed squarely at the one critique that is still open. It also means you walk into the room having thrown away nothing.
Sketch two · optional chapter one
Block Drop

The Candy Crush shaped one. You are dealt blocks from a cash tray and you drop them where you want. Then the market takes its turn, and a shock can delete an entire colour off the board.

Left: the board before the shock. Right: an energy shock has taken the orange column out. The player who spread across five colours lost the top of one stack. The player who went all in lost the stack.
A turn is one round:
  1. Three blocks arrive in the cash tray. They are not new money, they are your money in a different place.
  2. You drop them into whichever colour columns you want.
  3. The market resolves. Some columns grow a block, some lose one, and roughly one round in six a sector shock hits a whole colour.
  4. Clear the level by reaching a marked height before you run out of rounds.
Metaphor test
  • yesSame block language, so everything the re-staged Stack passes, this passes.
  • yesConcentration risk is felt, not stated: the shock erases what you piled up.
  • watchDropping blocks can read as creating value. Fix: blocks always come out of a visible cash tray that empties, so placing is moving, never making.
  • noTime is rounds, not dates. You lose the real price history, so this cannot be the game that teaches staying in the market.
Gamification test
  • yesThe most legibly game-like of the three. A six year old could open it.
  • yesLimited blocks and limited rounds. Two scarcities.
  • yesLevels, star ratings, the whole casual scaffold works here.
  • yesThe best onboarding: it teaches the block language before any money words appear.
  • watchReward function is the hazard. Any objective phrased as "use at least four colours" teaches obedience, not risk. The shock has to do the teaching, never a rule.
Verdict. The strongest answer to critique two and the weakest to critique one. Do not ship it as the game. Ship it as chapter one, the five minute thing that teaches a player to read a block before the game starts using real years.
Sketch three · optional finale
Forty Years

Your whole working life in one screen, and six trades to spend on all of it. The only real decision is whether to touch anything at all.

Two crashes, both survived. Four trades still unspent at year thirty-one. The green band is the automatic monthly contribution, which never asked permission and quietly became most of the chart.
A turn is a year, and most years you do nothing:
  1. The year plays out. You watch blocks stack.
  2. You may spend one of your six lifetime trades. The counter is always on screen.
  3. When a crash starts, the game offers you a very easy Sell Everything button and starts a short timer.
  4. At the end you see the run you had, and the run you would have had if you had left it alone.
Metaphor test
  • yesAll six, at the scale where compounding is finally visible as a shape.
  • yesThe single best picture of recovery: the notch fills back in and keeps going.
  • yesDollar cost averaging shows up as a band that thickens on its own. Nothing else visualises that well.
Gamification test
  • yesSix moves in forty years is the most extreme constraint of the three, and constraint is the pleasure.
  • watchObjective is soft. Needs a stated goal at the start, a number you retire on, or there is nothing to fail.
  • noNot much escalating difficulty. It is one long arc, so it works as an ending, not as a level system.
  • yesBest share artifact by far: one image, forty years, two crashes, and a number.
  • yesThe Sell Everything button is the whole curriculum. It is the fail state, and almost every kid presses it once.
Verdict. Not a standalone game, and it does not need to be. It is the last chapter, the one that pays off everything the earlier chapters taught, and it is the one that would actually get screenshotted.

What I would take into the room

The headline is that this is not a third pivot and not a third prototype. The game you already built at /stack stays exactly as it is. One canvas component gets swapped, and the thing Jared said was not foolproof becomes countable.

Say it in that order. The loop was never the open question after v3: the corridor, the stars and the gates already answer critique two. What is still open is critique one, and the answer is the oldest rule in visual public education, which is that you show a bigger quantity with more units and never with a bigger shape.

Then, if there is appetite for more, Block Drop is a five minute chapter one that teaches the block language before any money words appear, and Forty Years is a finale where the constraint becomes absolute and the lesson is that the correct move is almost always nothing. Both reuse the same stage. Neither is needed for the next meeting.

Build order

  • 1  TileStage.tsx with the same props as StackStage.tsx, half a day. Answer the block denomination question by feel on the real 2008 era. If it is ugly, the idea dies cheap and nothing else was touched.
  • 2  A stage toggle on the scenario page so the cylinder and the blocks run the same level side by side. That toggle is the demo.
  • 3  Send the reasoning to Jared before the meeting, so the meeting is about the loop instead of another defence of a picture.
  • 4  Ask for a paper review this round rather than a built demo. Polish keeps getting rejected on structure, and a paper prototype cannot be rejected on polish.
