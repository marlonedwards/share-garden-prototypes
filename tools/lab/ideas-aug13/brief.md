You are a veteran game designer hired by a small studio building a suite of small browser games that teach financial literacy the way SimCity teaches city planning: as a second-order effect of play, never as content delivery.

Design principles (from our design review, non-negotiable):

1. Fun first. Each game must be playable and enjoyable as a game by someone with zero interest in finance. Educational value must emerge from the mechanics, not from text.
2. No tutorials. A player learns by poking at the game, like a kid playing Pocket City who cannot read the menus yet still learns by dragging things around. If a mechanic needs explaining, redesign the mechanic.
3. Freedom to fail. Never constrain the player to the financially correct path and never lecture. Let them daytrade, over-concentrate, panic sell, borrow too much. Simulate honest consequences; do not designer-punish or block progress for bad practice. The consequence IS the lesson.
4. Real-time over turn-based. The world ticks continuously at compressed speed (like SimCity or tycoon games), with the player acting whenever they want, not in discrete turns.
5. Money must be physically intuitive: denominations like $1, $10, $100, $1,000 that a player can see and compare at a glance.
6. Small games, NYT Games model: 5 to 15 minute sessions, highly replayable, ONE core lesson per game. Across the suite we want coverage of: compounding, diversification, patience versus churn, budgeting and cash flow, risk versus reward, debt, market history, and "the news is already priced in."
7. Honesty rules: nothing the player does moves a market price; no reward for prediction or timing luck; if real market data is used, real numbers only.
8. Competition interests us. Everything we have sketched so far is solo, you-versus-yourself, and it feels flat. We believe rivalry could make lessons land harder (think Monopoly), but we already rejected on-the-nose ideas like "named rival trader characters at desks with visible P&L" as out-of-touch theater. We want competition that is genuinely fun as a mechanic. Simple scripted CPU opponents are fine; no live multiplayer, no LLM opponents.

Deliverables, in markdown:

## A. Five game concepts
Five concepts, each wildly different from the others (different genre skeletons, not five trading games). For each give:
- Name
- The core loop, described as a concrete 30 seconds of play
- Why it is fun: the itch it scratches and the closest existing hit game
- The ONE financial lesson, and the exact mechanic through which that lesson emerges without a single word of explanation
- How a player can play badly, and what they see happen
- The real-time element
- A competitive variant with scripted CPU opponents, if one fits naturally

## B. Five rivalry mechanics
Five competition mechanics for money games with scripted CPU opponents, each grounded in a proven fun pattern from a hit game (examples of patterns: Monopoly's property scarcity, auction pressure, Mario Kart's rubber banding, ghost racing, shared-pot brinkmanship, territory control, asynchronous leaderboards). For each give:
- How it works mechanically
- Why it is fun (which hit game proves the pattern)
- What financial lesson emerges from the competition itself, without words

## C. Three steals
Three existing commercial games (any genre, any platform) that teach a real skill inadvertently. For each: the specific mechanic that does the teaching, and a concrete transplant of that mechanic into a money game.

Constraints: be mechanical and concrete, not thematic or hand-wavy. Assume a two-person team can prototype each game in a week in a web canvas. No VR, no AR, no blockchain, no gacha, no ad-driven design. Do not include tutorials in any design. Do not pitch gamified quizzes or flashcards under any framing.
