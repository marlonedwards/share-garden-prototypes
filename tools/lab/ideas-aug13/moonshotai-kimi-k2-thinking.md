## A. Five Game Concepts

### 1. Bloom (Compounding)
**The core loop:** Bills ($10, $50, $100) drift down from the top of the screen like falling leaves. You drag them into one of four garden plots at the bottom. Once planted, a bill sprouts tiny leaves. After 20 seconds of real time, it splits into two identical bills (the original and a clone). Meanwhile, "Opportunity" cards scroll across the top—a bicycle for $150, a coffee for $5—each with a 10-second countdown. To buy, you must uproot bills from the soil (killing any future clones) and drag them to the card. Unspent opportunities vanish.

**Why it is fun:** Slot-machine anticipation married to gardening satisfaction. The pleasure of watching money literally reproduce. Closest hit: *Animal Crossing* meets *Cookie Clicker*.

**The ONE lesson:** Compounding is biological growth, not arithmetic. The mechanic: bills only duplicate if left untouched in the soil; spending requires destruction of the reproductive asset. No text explains this—you simply see your wallet empty if you spend and your garden overflow if you wait.

**How to play badly:** Buy every gadget the instant you have the cash. You never see a bill split; your garden stays barren. Or hoard forever, watch opportunities expire, and end with a mountain of unusable paper.

**Real-time element:** Bills grow continuously at their own biological clock; opportunities scroll on a conveyor; the garden ticks regardless of player input.

**Competition variant:** Three gardens side by side. Opportunities are shared—if you buy the bike, it is gone for the CPU opponents. You see their gardens flourishing or barren, creating FOMO pressure to spend prematurely.

### 2. Tributaries (Diversification)
**The core loop:** Five colored streams flow down the screen at different angles and speeds. Each emits coins: the yellow stream gushes large gold coins but randomly runs dry for 30 seconds; the blue trickles small silver coins steadily; the red spits rare giant coins unpredictably. You drag a bucket under any stream to catch what falls. The bucket has weight physics—if you park under one stream too long and it floods, the bucket tips and spills everything. You must physically move the bucket between streams to balance the catch.

**Why it is fun:** Physical catching satisfaction and chaos management. The twitch of dodging a dry spell. Closest hit: *Fruit Ninja* meets *Dyson Sphere Program*.

**The ONE lesson:** Smoothing returns through uncorrelated sources. The mechanic: streams have negatively correlated variance (when yellow dries, blue flows). A full bucket requires sampling all colors; concentration causes physical spillage when the single source inevitably chokes.

**How to play badly:** Park under the yellow "gold rush" stream permanently. It dries the moment you look away, you catch nothing for half a minute while other colors flow past uncollected, and your bucket remains empty.

**Real-time element:** Particle flow is continuous; variance cycles run on independent timers.

**Competition variant:** Shared river—coins caught by CPU opponents are gone for you. You see their buckets positioning; if they park on blue, you must choose between fighting for scraps (crowding) or moving to an empty stream (diversification).

### 3. Friction (Patience vs. Churn)
**The core loop:** Three vertical glass tubes contain rising colored liquids (assets). Each wanders randomly but drifts upward at 5% per minute. You possess a "pump" tool. Click tube A, then tube B: a physical $1 bill flies out of your cash tray and vanishes forever, transferring liquid to equalize the levels. The liquid keeps rising. You feel the urge to constantly rebalance so the "leader" tube doesn't get ahead, but each click costs visible money.

**Why it is fun:** The OCD satisfaction of tidying resisted by a tangible tax. Closest hit: *Tetris* meets *Overcooked*.

**The ONE lesson:** Transaction costs destroy alpha. The mechanic: the pump levies a real $1 fee per use, visibly deducted from your pile. The drift is random; chasing the leader guarantees paying fees for zero benefit, and the pile of spent $1 bills on the floor grows while your total liquid level lags behind a passive holder.

**How to play badly:** Rebalance every 5 seconds to "optimize" the mix. You burn $12/minute in fees. After 5 minutes, the static player has $60 more liquid than you despite identical underlying growth.

**Real-time element:** Liquids rise and wander continuously; fees deduct instantly.

**Competition variant:** Ghost racing. A semi-transparent overlay shows the run of a "HODL" CPU who never moves liquid. You watch their total value slowly overtake yours as you waste money on friction.

### 4. Clockwork (Budgeting and Cash Flow)
**The core loop:** You are a desk with seven slots (days). Income (stacks of bills) arrives at the top on random days. Fixed expenses (invoices stamped "RENT," "PHONE") appear on the right with countdown timers showing when they auto-deduct. You drag bills from income into the day-slots to reserve them for specific invoices. If a timer hits zero and the cash isn't there, the invoice grows larger (late fee) and turns red. You may also drag cash to a "Vault" tile that locks it for 7 days but returns it doubled—however, while locked it is invisible to the auto-deduct system, causing defaults.

**Why it is fun:** Spatial juggling and brinkmanship. Closest hit: *Overcooked* meets *Papers, Please*.

**The ONE lesson:** Liquidity is physical availability, not ownership. The mechanic: money in the vault is physically removed from the desk; the auto-deduct vacuum cannot reach it, causing cascading failure if you over-invest. You learn to keep a "float" in the open slots.

**How to play badly:** Vault every dollar immediately to maximize returns. Rent comes due, the vacuum finds nothing, your desk flashes red, and penalties stack. Or spend everything on day one, leaving empty slots for future bills.

**Real-time element:** Clock ticks continuously; auto-deduct happens whether you are ready or not; vaults unlock on their own schedule.

**Competition variant:** Survival race. You and a CPU receive identical income shocks. Who lasts longer as expense frequency accelerates? You see their desk layout and learn from their liquidity buffers.

### 5. Lag (The News is Already Priced In)
**The core loop:** A price line scrolls continuously leftward across the screen. Above it, a news ticker displays headlines ("Earnings Up!", "CEO Quits!") with a 3-second delay from the actual price move. You have BUY and SELL buttons. When you click BUY, your entry point is marked on the chart. If you buy on the "Earnings Up!" headline, you enter after the spike has already occurred; the price immediately stagnates or reverses because the move happened 3 seconds ago (the "wire" you cannot see).

**Why it is fun:** Pattern-recognition trap; rhythm game where the beat lies to you. Closest hit: *Audiosurf* meets *Guitar Hero* (with broken audio sync).

**The ONE lesson:** You trade on stale information. The mechanic: the visual headline is explicitly delayed; the chart leads the text. Buying the "good news" consistently buys the top, and your P&L line (shown in the corner) bleeds downward until you realize the causality is reversed.

**How to play badly:** React instantly to every headline. You buy highs and sell lows, your cash tray empties, and the chart mocks you with "should have bought here" ghost marks.

**Real-time element:** Price moves continuously; headlines emit on a timer offset from the price algorithm.

**Competition variant:** Three traders with different lag times. You (3 sec), CPU A (0 sec, "insider"), CPU B (5 sec, "retail"). You see their entry marks appear before/after yours, teaching the cost of information latency.

## B. Five Rivalry Mechanics

### 1. Desperation Rubber-Banding (Mario Kart Blue Shell)
**How it works:** CPU opponents trailing the player receive invisible "desperation" buffs—access to high-leverage instruments (margin) that the leader cannot use. When behind, they take bigger risks that sometimes pay off exponentially, keeping scores tight until the final minute.

**Why it is fun:** Maintains tension; prevents runaway victories. Proven by *Mario Kart*.

**Financial lesson:** Risk increases with desperation. The trailing CPU's volatile swings demonstrate that leveraged recovery attempts often blow up or miraculously work, teaching that late-stage risk-taking is emotionally driven, not mathematically sound.

---

### 2. Scarcity Auction (Monopoly Property)
**How it works:** Three assets appear per round. All players (you + 2 CPUs) submit sealed bids simultaneously. Winner pays immediately from cash tray. Unsold assets depreciate and vanish. You must balance liquidity (cash on hand) against winning bids.

**Why it is fun:** Auction tension and winner's curse. Proven by *Monopoly* and *Power Grid*.

**Financial lesson:** Liquidity premium. The competition itself teaches that overpaying in competitive environments (bidding wars) destroys returns, while holding cash reserves allows snatching distressed assets later.

---

### 3. Ghost Strategy Overlay (Trackmania)
**How it works:** The CPU's previous winning run plays as a semi-transparent overlay on your screen. You see exactly when they bought, sold, or held, and how their net worth diverged from yours at each decision point.

**Why it is fun:** Direct comparison; learning by mimicry; "oh, so that's when they pivoted." Proven by *Trackmania* and *Trials*.

**Financial lesson:** Path dependence. Watching the ghost reveals that early small divergences (fees, timing) compound into massive gaps later, teaching the irreversibility of financial decisions.

---

### 4. Escalating Ante Brinkmanship (Poker)
**How it works:** A central pot grows continuously. A random timer (5–15 seconds) resets unpredictably. Players contribute to the pot to stay in. If the timer stops while you are the last to have contributed, you win the pot. Risk: contribute too early and you waste money if someone else wins; wait too long and you get shut out.

**Why it is fun:** Chicken mechanic; timing pressure; "hot potato." Proven by *Poker* and *Spoons*.

**Financial lesson:** Cost of waiting. The pot represents inflation or opportunity cost. The mechanic forces realization that delaying decisions has a real price, and market timing is indistinguishable from luck.

---

### 5. Majority Territory (Carcassonne)
**How it works:** A grid represents market sectors. Players place limited capital tokens (meeples) into sectors. When a sector "pays out," the majority holder receives the full dividend; minority holders receive nothing. You must choose between crowding a popular sector (risking being minority) or pioneering empty sectors (risking no payout at all).

**Why it is fun:** Area control; efficient placement; blocking. Proven by *Carcassonne* and *El Grande*.

**Financial lesson:** Crowding and winner-take-all markets. The competition itself teaches that popular trades (crowded sectors) offer diminishing returns because the pie is split, while contrarian positions carry binary risk (all or nothing).

## C. Three Steals

### 1. Papers, Please – The End-of-Day Budget Slider
**The specific mechanic:** After each workday, a screen displays your income as physical cash. Below, sliders represent fixed costs (rent, heat, food, medicine). You drag cash to each slider; whatever is left remains. Your family’s status icons (hungry, sick, cold) update instantly based on your allocation. No numbers are spoken; the visual state of the room changes (heater on/off, medicine present/absent).

**Transplant into money game:** "Settlement." Every 2 minutes (game month), the screen freezes. You drag bills from an "Income" pile into envelopes labeled "Due," "Invest," and "Spend." An avatar in the corner wears rags if you under-invest, looks stressed if you over-invest and can't pay rent, or relaxes if you balance correctly. The lesson—budgeting as physical allocation—emerges from the drag-and-drop constraint, not a spreadsheet.

---

### 2. Mini Metro – Station Overload
**The specific mechanic:** Stations are shapes; passengers are shapes; mismatches cause backup. If a station overflows (too many waiting passengers), the game ends. You draw lines between stations to route supply (trains) to demand, but you have limited lines and tunnels.

**Transplant into money game:** "Duration." Assets are colored stations generating cash (circle, square, triangle). Liabilities are drains requiring specific shapes (mortgage needs squares, groceries need circles). You draw lines (capital allocation) between them. If a liability station overflows (unpaid for 10 seconds), it explodes and ends the run. You learn cash-flow matching: long-term assets (trains) cannot solve short-term liabilities (stations) if the line is too long (duration mismatch).

---

### 3. Vampire Survivors – Evolution Grid
**The specific mechanic:** Weapons level up by collecting experience gems. When a weapon maxes out and you hold a specific passive item, the weapon evolves into a super-form (e.g., Whip + Hollow Heart = Bloody Tear). Early choices lock in late-game potential; incompatible choices waste slots.

**Transplant into money game:** "Syndrome." You have a 2x2 grid. Placing a "Stock" token in slot (1,1) and a "Bond" token in slot (1,2) creates a "Balanced Portfolio" syndrome that auto-collects interest (passive income). Placing two "Tech Stock" tokens creates "Concentration Risk"—high damage but random stun-lock (liquidity freeze). The mechanic teaches that asset combinations produce emergent properties (diversification benefits) that single assets cannot, and that early concentration blocks late-game synergies.
