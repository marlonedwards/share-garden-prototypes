## A. Five game concepts

These are intentionally different genre skeletons. Prices, shocks, and customer demand are generated by the game or replayed from fixed historical data; player actions never move a market price.

### 1. **Orchard Rush**

- **Name:** Orchard Rush
- **Core loop — 30 seconds of play:**  
  Drag a $10 seedling onto an empty plot. It grows in real time through three visible stages. At harvest, the player can:
  - take the fruit as cash,
  - plant a second tree,
  - or buy a faster-growing variety.  
  The player is constantly deciding whether to collect a small pile now or leave a larger living orchard running.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  It scratches the “watch my machine get bigger” itch: planting, upgrading, harvesting, and optimizing space. Closest hits are **Cookie Clicker**, **Forager**, and the farm loop in **Stardew Valley**.
- **The ONE financial lesson:**  
  **Reinvested gains can themselves produce gains.**
- **Exact mechanic that teaches it:**  
  Each tree produces fruit proportional to its current size. Harvesting removes the tree’s accumulated growth; reinvesting fruit creates more trees whose growth runs simultaneously. The orchard’s total output visibly accelerates as more gains remain invested.
- **How a player can play badly:**  
  They harvest every tree the moment it produces anything, leaving a field of tiny stumps. Their cash pile looks healthy, but the orchard’s fruit counter crawls while another player’s orchard fills the screen. No warning appears; the difference is visible in production.
- **Real-time element:**  
  Trees grow continuously, weather changes growth speed, and harvest windows pass while the player is doing other actions.
- **Competitive variant:**  
  Every player and CPU receives the same plot shape and weather seed. CPUs appear as translucent “ghost orchards.” The winner is whoever has the most combined cash and living orchard value after ten minutes. The rivalry is against visible growth curves, not against a fictional trader.

### 2. **Convoy**

- **Name:** Convoy
- **Core loop — 30 seconds of play:**  
  The player has $1,000 represented by physical $1, $10, $100, and $1,000 cargo tiles. They buy and dispatch small delivery vehicles:
  - a cheap coastal boat,
  - a slower but stable rail route,
  - a volatile mountain truck,
  - and a refrigerated air route.  
  Each route delivers automatically. The player continually moves cash between routes, replaces damaged vehicles, and decides whether to add another vehicle to an existing route or open a different route.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  It scratches the logistics and fleet-optimization itch. Closest hits are **Mini Metro**, **Railroad Tycoon**, and **FTL**’s crew-and-system triage.
- **The ONE financial lesson:**  
  **Spreading exposure makes one bad outcome less destructive.**
- **Exact mechanic that teaches it:**  
  Storms, landslides, fuel shortages, and demand drops affect routes independently. A route can have a better average payout but a larger chance of going offline. The player’s total delivery income is the sum of all active routes, so concentration creates a visibly fragile convoy.
- **How a player can play badly:**  
  They put every dollar into mountain trucks. A landslide closes the route. Their whole income stream stops, repair bills arrive, and the player must liquidate vehicles to continue. A diversified convoy keeps earning through the same event.
- **Real-time element:**  
  Vehicles travel continuously, route incidents happen during deliveries, and maintenance costs tick while the player is making allocation decisions.
- **Competitive variant:**  
  CPUs use the same weather and demand sequence but choose different fleet mixes: concentrated, evenly spread, and “high-risk.” Their convoys are visible on the same map. The player learns diversification by watching one CPU suffer a total outage while another keeps moving.

### 3. **Night Shift**

- **Name:** Night Shift
- **Core loop — 30 seconds of play:**  
  Run a tiny diner during a continuous five-minute night. Customers arrive in waves. The player:
  - buys ingredients with visible denominations,
  - assigns workers,
  - serves orders,
  - pays wages and rent as clocked bills,
  - and decides whether to keep a cash buffer or spend it on capacity.  
  Every few seconds, cash physically moves between the till, supplier shelf, payroll tray, and landlord tray.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  It scratches the frantic optimization itch of **Overcooked!**, **Diner Dash**, and **PlateUp!** The pleasure comes from getting a messy operation flowing smoothly.
- **The ONE financial lesson:**  
  **Being profitable is not the same as having enough cash at the moment a bill arrives.**
- **Exact mechanic that teaches it:**  
  Revenue arrives unevenly, but wages, rent, and supplier invoices have fixed real-time due moments. A player may have a profitable menu and still fail if cash is tied up in ingredients or upgrades when payroll hits.
- **How a player can play badly:**  
  They spend the entire till on a second grill. The diner earns more per minute, but payroll arrives before the extra sales accumulate. Workers leave, orders pile up, and the player has to shut stations while a large, visibly profitable restaurant collapses.
- **Real-time element:**  
  Customers, cooking, wages, inventory spoilage, and bills all continue while the player acts. There are no turns or pauses required to make a financial decision.
- **Competitive variant:**  
  A CPU diner serves the same customer stream but has a different operating style: cheap-and-steady, expansion-heavy, or cash-hoarding. The player sees each restaurant’s open/closed stations and cash trays. The CPU is not a financial advisor; it is simply another operating strategy to beat.

### 4. **Launch Window**

- **Name:** Launch Window
- **Core loop — 30 seconds of play:**  
  Build and launch a small rocket. Components sit on a conveyor: engines, fuel tanks, navigation units, and payloads. The player can buy components with cash, or borrow a stack of physical debt chips to assemble a more powerful rocket immediately. Once launched, the rocket automatically travels through a sequence of hazards and returns a payout if it survives. Interest ticks continuously on every unpaid loan.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  It scratches the “make a machine, then see if my risky plan works” itch. Closest hits are **Kerbal Space Program**, **FTL**, and **Into the Breach**’s compact risk assessment.
- **The ONE financial lesson:**  
  **Borrowing can accelerate growth, but fixed repayment costs make failure more dangerous.**
- **Exact mechanic that teaches it:**  
  Debt increases available launch power immediately, but interest is deducted continuously whether or not the rocket earns money. A failed launch still leaves the loan alive. Successful launches can repay debt faster, creating a clear leverage upside and downside.
- **How a player can play badly:**  
  They borrow $1,000 to buy the strongest engine, then borrow another $1,000 to add payload. The rocket fails, interest drains the workshop, and future launches become weaker because the player must service old debt. A smaller, cash-funded launch may grow more slowly but remain operational.
- **Real-time element:**  
  Interest accumulates every second, component prices on the conveyor expire, and the assembled rocket launches automatically after a short countdown.
- **Competitive variant:**  
  The player and three CPUs share the same sequence of component offers and launch conditions. Each CPU has a recognizable policy: no debt, moderate debt, or maximum debt. The winner is the workshop with the most net cash after eight minutes, not the largest single launch.

### 5. **Already Gone**

- **Name:** Already Gone
- **Core loop — 30 seconds of play:**  
  The player runs a fast, tactile “market history” machine using a fixed replay of real historical daily data. Asset lanes scroll from left to right. A price move appears first as a colored bar; a newspaper icon or broadcast arrives a few seconds later. The player can place money into broad asset baskets before the replay begins and can rebalance during the run, but cannot alter the bars. New deposits and withdrawals are represented by physical cash chips.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  It scratches the pattern-recognition and route-planning itch of **Mini Metro**, **Thumper**, and arcade “survive the sequence” games. The fun is in constructing a robust route through a dramatic, immutable replay rather than correctly guessing a future price.
- **The ONE financial lesson:**  
  **By the time widely available news explains a price move, the move is already reflected in the price.**
- **Exact mechanic that teaches it:**  
  Every headline is delayed until after the corresponding historical price movement. A player who waits for the headline receives no better price than a player who acted before it; often they receive a worse one. The sequence uses real historical numbers rather than invented “correct answers.”
- **How a player can play badly:**  
  They see a large red move, wait for the newspaper explanation, then sell. The sell animation occurs after the drop has already happened. On replay, they can see that the headline was not a usable early signal.
- **Real-time element:**  
  The historical tape, headlines, transaction costs, and cash needs progress continuously. The player may act at any time, but cannot rewind the replay.
- **Competitive variant:**  
  CPUs receive the same historical replay. One is a headline-chaser, one rebalances frequently, and one sets a simple allocation and leaves it alone. A ghost replay shows when each CPU acted, while the final score is based on ending wealth after costs. This makes “reaction” compete against “staying invested” without rewarding a lucky invented forecast.

## B. Five rivalry mechanics

### 1. **The Finite Block Auction**

- **How it works mechanically:**  
  The board contains a fixed number of productive plots, warehouses, or toll bridges. Players and CPUs bid for them using physical cash stacks. Once claimed, a plot cannot be duplicated. Each plot produces a known but uneven stream of income. Auctions happen in real time when a plot becomes available; bids can be raised until the countdown expires.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  This is the property scarcity and rent engine proven by **Monopoly**. A finite board creates immediate rivalry: the opponent is not merely scoring more; they are taking the thing you wanted.
- **Financial lesson emerging from competition:**  
  **The price paid for an asset matters; owning something desirable does not make any price sensible.**
- **Why it is educational without words:**  
  A player can win a valuable plot but have no cash left for repairs, bills, or better opportunities. The opponent’s income may be smaller but more liquid.

---

### 2. **The Shared-Pot Countdown**

- **How it works mechanically:**  
  A central pot fills continuously with cash while a visible hazard meter rises. At any moment, each player can “bank” their current share. Banking removes them from the round. If the hazard triggers first, everyone still in loses the unbanked portion. The pot grows faster when more players remain in.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  This is the push-your-luck pattern from **Can’t Stop**, **Farkle**, and many **Mario Party** minigames. The fun is social-looking brinkmanship, even against simple CPUs: “Will they cash out before I do?”
- **Financial lesson emerging from competition:**  
  **A larger possible reward comes with a larger chance of losing what has already been accumulated.**
- **Why it is educational without words:**  
  The player watches a CPU bank early and survive with a modest stack while the player stays in and loses a much larger unbanked stack. No moral or probability explanation is needed.

---

### 3. **Ghost Allocation Racing**

- **How it works mechanically:**  
  Everyone receives the same deterministic sequence of external events. Each player chooses when to allocate, withdraw, rebalance, or pay transaction costs. The game records every action and replays CPU decisions as translucent ghosts on the same timeline.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  This is the ghost-racing pattern from **TrackMania**, **Mario Kart Time Trials**, and **Beat Saber** leaderboards. The opponent is always visible, but never blocks or sabotages the player.
- **Financial lesson emerging from competition:**  
  **Frequent action has a cost, and a simple strategy can beat frantic activity.**
- **Why it is educational without words:**  
  The ghost that makes ten reallocations visibly loses small amounts to fees and slippage. The quiet ghost remains in the race and may finish ahead even when it never appears to make a clever move.

---

### 4. **The Reserve Territory**

- **How it works mechanically:**  
  A map contains territories that generate fixed income but occasionally require a repair, replacement, or emergency payment. Players claim territories by moving workers into them. A player can own multiple territories, but each worker and repair crew can only service one location at a time. CPUs compete for the same map.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  It combines the claiming and area-control pattern of **Risk**, **Splendor**, and **Civilization** with the visible spatial pressure of **Mini Metro**. Rivalry is concrete: the CPU is taking useful places on the board.
- **Financial lesson emerging from competition:**  
  **A reserve is useful because it gives you options when the unexpected happens.**
- **Why it is educational without words:**  
  A player who spends every cash chip claiming territory may own the largest area but be unable to repair it. A smaller player with an untouched reserve can acquire abandoned territory and keep operating.

---

### 5. **The Catch-Up Contract**

- **How it works mechanically:**  
  Players compete to complete a fixed set of contracts. The player currently in first receives more expensive or less convenient contract offers; trailing CPUs receive slightly easier routes or a small cash rebate. The catch-up is visible and deterministic, not hidden. Contracts have fixed payouts and costs; no player changes any price.
- **Why it is fun:**  
  This uses the rubber-banding pattern proven by **Mario Kart** and **Mario Tennis**. A race remains tense instead of becoming decided halfway through.
- **Financial lesson emerging from competition:**  
  **Having more money does not automatically mean having more freedom; commitments and costs can make a lead fragile.**
- **Why it is educational without words:**  
  The leader is given larger contracts but must front more cash and manage more obligations. A careful trailing player can pass by remaining liquid, while a reckless leader becomes unable to accept the next opportunity.
- **Design caution:**  
  Rubber-banding should affect contract availability and CPU pacing, never market prices or the player’s earned returns. The player must be able to see the cause of the changing pressure through the board state.

## C. Three steals

### 1. **Tetris → Physical cash packing**

- **What the original game teaches:**  
  Tetris teaches spatial planning, gap avoidance, and the future cost of making a locally attractive move.
- **Specific mechanic:**  
  Falling pieces must be rotated and placed into a constrained grid. A move that fills the current hole can create an unusable cavity later.
- **Concrete transplant into a money game:**  
  Give the player a small “cash drawer” grid with fixed slots. $1, $10, $100, and $1,000 bills occupy different physical footprints. Incoming revenue, bills, debt repayments, and emergency expenses must be packed into the drawer.  
  A player can break larger bills, but breaking them takes time and incurs a small fixed handling cost. A drawer filled with awkward denominations may technically contain enough total money but fail to pay an exact bill on time.  
  This can become a compact cash-flow game without displaying a budget lesson or asking arithmetic questions.

---

### 2. **Mini Metro → Bottleneck management**

- **What the original game teaches:**  
  Mini Metro teaches that a system’s failure is often caused by a bottleneck rather than by insufficient total capacity.
- **Specific mechanic:**  
  Passengers accumulate at stations, lines share constrained trains and tunnels, and one overloaded interchange can destabilize the whole network.
- **Concrete transplant into a money game:**  
  Build a live business with separate cash-flow stations:
  - sales,
  - inventory,
  - payroll,
  - taxes,
  - maintenance,
  - debt service.  
  Cash travels along visible pipes between stations. The player can add workers, shorten routes, or place a temporary cash buffer at a junction. Total cash may be positive, but a clogged receivables pipe can leave payroll empty.  
  A CPU business uses a different pipe layout. The player competes to remain operational longest, learning cash timing through the network’s physical congestion.

---

### 3. **Into the Breach → Telegraphing and commitment**

- **What the original game teaches:**  
  Into the Breach teaches planning around visible future consequences. The player is not rewarded for guessing hidden information; they are rewarded for responding well to a forecast.
- **Specific mechanic:**  
  Enemy attacks are shown before they resolve. The player can change positioning and mitigate damage, but cannot erase every threat.
- **Concrete transplant into a money game:**  
  Run a real-time workshop where upcoming obligations appear as physically approaching objects:
  - payroll truck,
  - tax truck,
  - loan-payment drone,
  - replacement-parts shipment.  
  Their amounts and arrival times are visible. The player can rearrange production, reserve cash, refinance, or cancel a project, but each action has a delay. CPUs face the same obligation stream.  
  The lesson emerges from commitment: a player who accepts a large obligation without leaving room for the already-visible future payment eventually gets hit by it. The game teaches planning and debt awareness without a financial explanation screen.
