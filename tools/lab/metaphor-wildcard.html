The Ladder Yard

A company you own is a ladder standing in your yard. Its value is the number of rungs, and rungs are evenly spaced, so height and count say the same thing. You can read it across the room and you can also literally count it. An index fund is not a ladder at all: it is a staircase built from one step of every company in the market.

Why this beats the glass orb

The orb encoded value as area, which the eye cannot compare and cannot count, so growth and decline disappeared into the mix. A ladder encodes value as a stack of identical units. Adding a rung is growth. Taking one off is decline. Nothing is inferred; the change happens at the top of the ladder where the eye already is.

It also carries the three things bars and coin stacks cannot: a ladder can snap (bankruptcy is a different state, not a lower number), a ladder can lean in the wind (a market-wide shock is one gust hitting the whole yard), and a ladder is a thing a kid stands up, folds down, and carries, which gives buying and selling real bodies.

The index reads as the steady hero for a structural reason, not a promised one: a staircase is wide at the bottom and cannot tip, and if one company fails, that step just turns gray while the staircase keeps its shape and still holds your weight. It is drawn as the calmest thing in the yard, never the tallest.

Considered and rejected: water tanks and aquariums (a level is volume again, the orb's exact mistake, and a half-full tank looks the same as a broken one), bookshelves and trains (horizontal counts that are just coin stacks turned sideways, with nothing to say about failure), candles (they only ever go down, so growth is impossible), mountains (nobody buys, sells or breaks a mountain), snowmen and sandcastles (melting implies time alone destroys value). No chart lines appear anywhere in this file: the yard shows today only, so nothing on screen can be read as a forecast.
